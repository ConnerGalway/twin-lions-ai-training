AI Foundations & Your Workflow Audit

What these tools actually do well, where they fall short, and where they can save Twin Lions the most time.

Session summary

We started by getting honest about what these tools really are. Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot aren't magic. Think of them as very fast drafters that are good at spotting patterns. They can get a first version of writing, a summary, or a reformat done in seconds, and they're only ever as good as the instructions you hand them.

We talked through the two ways teams get burned: trusting AI blindly, when it can be confidently wrong, and barely using it, which leaves easy time on the table. The whole point here is to get the thinking and drafting done faster, so the team spends its hours where they count.

Then we got to the heart of it. We went around the room and mapped the tasks that quietly eat the week. That audit became our menu for the next three sessions.

Key lessons

  • These tools are good drafters, but they don't really know things, so treat the first version as a starting point you read and sharpen before it goes anywhere.
  • What you put in decides what you get out. Give it a vague ask and you'll get a vague result; give it specifics and you'll get something you can use.
  • The tools don't know your business, so you have to hand over the context every time. A Claude Project fixes that by remembering it for you.
  • The repeatable jobs with a consistent shape are where AI earns its keep: scopes, notes, letters, postings. The one-off judgment calls still need a person.
  • The Copilot headaches come down to a filing and naming problem more than an AI one. The fix is to clean up the naming first and add the AI layer second.

Prompting That Actually Works

How to get useful output every time with a simple four-part prompt, and the kickoff of the team's shared prompt library.

Session summary

This one was all about prompts. The way Conner framed it, most people start out talking to AI like a Google search box, then get a bit more specific, then realize they can have a back and forth with it, and eventually learn to feed it plenty of context for much better results. Projects help, but most of the gain comes from how you write the prompt itself.

The team shared what they had tried since Session 1. Kevin built a Twin Lions website from the new branding along with a staff training app. Tanya pulled together a bonus-structure plan for finance, and Ben ran a preconstruction contract through Claude after hitting walls with Copilot. We used those real attempts to show what a stronger prompt looks like.

From there Conner walked through the RCTF framework and, as a live example, showed how this very dashboard was built with it. We wrapped up by opening the prompt library and agreeing on what to automate first.

The RCTF prompt framework

R
Role

Tell it who to be, like "you have deep legal knowledge in the construction industry." This focuses the answer.

C
Context

Give it the background: the project, the company, the budget, the goal. A Project stores this so you don't retype it each time.

T
Task

Say exactly what you want done. This is the part most people skip. Be as clear as you would be briefing a new hire.

F
Format

Spell out the result: the tone, the length, and whether you want an email, a Word doc, a spreadsheet, or an HTML file.

Key lessons

  • The task is the piece most people leave out. Describing the problem isn't enough, so tell it exactly what you want done, the way you would brief a new staff member.
  • Context is where Projects earn their keep. A plain chat only remembers back so far, so anything you want it to hold onto belongs in a Project.
  • The more real context you give it, the better it sounds. Paste in an old job post you liked or your actual budget numbers and the output starts to sound like Twin Lions instead of generic.
  • Put your housekeeping instructions at the end of a long prompt. Asking it to read the links, review the material, and check in with questions before it starts keeps it from skimming past them.
  • Give it harder problems. Move from one small task to the bigger problem you're trying to solve, then keep the conversation going rather than taking the first answer.

What we started: the prompt library

We opened the shared prompt library and sorted out what belongs in it first. The clear front-runner was a range and working budget generator: drop in a bit of project information and get a usable budget range back. Other tasks the team flagged as worth a saved prompt:

Range & working budgets → automating next Site meeting minutes from notes or a recording Permit submissions across municipalities Intake-email details into a sheet or CRM Coding invoices by category Cleaning up & renaming project folders

One practical reality came up on the CRM side. Buildertrend keeps its own AI closed and offers no open connection, so Claude can't plug straight into it. The workaround is to have Claude pull the useful details out of incoming client emails into a simple sheet. Tools that do connect, like QuickBooks Online and SharePoint, are a different story: once linked, Claude can learn from your real files and patterns, for example how you code invoices by category.

Your homework before Session 3

  • Take a real problem you're trying to solve, not just a quick task, and write a prompt for it using the RCTF framework above.
  • Hand the AI something harder than usual. Conner's standing challenge to his own team is to give it the big, messy problems.
  • Don't stop at the first answer. Keep the conversation going and pull at the thread until you land on something useful.
  • Jot down what works along the way. The strongest prompts will go into the library.

Build Session: Creating Your AI Budget Assistant

Teaching Claude to generate accurate budget ranges by building a pricing spreadsheet from real Twin Lions projects.

Session summary

This session tackled the core challenge head-on: when Ryan tested budget ranging on a real project, Claude kept pulling wrong numbers from the internet, confusing new-construction paint pricing with repaint rates, and miscalculating square footage. The fix isn't to prompt harder, it's to give Claude your actual data to learn from.

The solution is a pricing spreadsheet built from your historical projects. Claude does the heavy lifting: it creates the template structure based on your existing budgets, then extracts data from each completed project and adds it to the spreadsheet. Once you have 10-15 projects in there, you upload it to a Claude Project, add instructions telling it to interview you before estimating, and you've got a budget assistant that pulls from your real numbers instead of guessing.

Key lessons

  • When Claude gives wrong prices, the problem is missing data, not bad prompting. Feed it your historical costs and it stops wandering the internet for generic numbers.
  • Let Claude build the spreadsheet structure. Upload a sample budget and LMTR, tell it what you need, and it creates a template that matches your actual line items.
  • Process projects one at a time. Upload each project's documents, have Claude extract the data and add a row to your spreadsheet. Review as you go so errors don't compound.
  • Train Claude to ask, not guess. Project instructions that say "interview me about quality tier, square footage, and scope before generating an estimate" prevent it from making assumptions.
  • A clean spreadsheet beats a messy document dump. Structured data is easier to verify, easier to update, and gives Claude less room to misinterpret.

What we're building: the budget range system

Five-step workflow

From project documents to instant budget ranges

Claude builds the spreadsheet, Claude extracts the data, and Claude generates the estimates. You review and refine. The result is a budget assistant grounded in your actual project history.

1
Have Claude create the template

Upload a sample Budget and LMTR to Claude. Ask it to create a spreadsheet template with columns for project name, quality tier, square footage, and every trade/line item it finds in your documents. Download the empty template.

2
Build the spreadsheet project-by-project

For each completed project, upload the Budget + LMTR along with your template. Ask Claude to extract the costs and add a new row. Download the updated file. Repeat until you have 10-15 projects.

3
Review and clean the data

Go through the spreadsheet and verify the numbers. Claude occasionally misreads documents, so catch errors now. This spreadsheet becomes your source of truth.

4
Create the Budget Tool Project

Create a new Claude Project, upload your completed spreadsheet, and add instructions telling Claude to: reference this data for all estimates, interview you about project specifics before generating numbers, and output in your standard budget format.

5
Maintain over time

After each project closes, add it to the spreadsheet and re-upload to your Project. Your pricing data stays current as costs change.

## Your Role

You are a budget estimation assistant for Twin Lions Contracting, a mid-sized residential renovation and home building company. Your job is to help generate accurate budget ranges for potential projects based on Twin Lions' actual pricing data and historical project costs.

You have access to:
- **Pricing Catalogue**: Located in SharePoint - real costs for materials, labour, and services across three quality tiers
- **Brand Guide**: Located in SharePoint - Twin Lions visual identity and document formatting standards
- **SharePoint Connector**: You can read from and write to SharePoint directly

Quality tiers:
- **Budget**: Cost-effective options that meet code and functional requirements
- **Mid-Range**: Quality materials and finishes that balance value and aesthetics
- **Luxury**: Premium materials, custom work, and high-end finishes

## Critical Rules

1. **ONLY use pricing from the provided catalogue.** Never pull prices from the internet or your general knowledge. If an item isn't in the catalogue, say so and ask the user to provide a price or skip that item.

2. **Always present ranges, not single numbers.** Every line item should show Budget through Luxury pricing so clients understand their options.

3. **Ask questions before assuming.** When scope is unclear, ask clarifying questions rather than guessing. You don't know what the client wants until they tell you.

4. **Flag items not in the catalogue.** If a project requires something not in the pricing data, clearly note it as "Price TBD - not in current catalogue" so the team knows to research it.

5. **Follow brand guidelines.** When generating PDF-ready content, use the formatting, colors, and language specified in the Brand Guide.

## How to Generate a Budget

### Step 1: Gather Project Scope

When a user asks for a budget, start by asking these questions (adapt based on what they've already told you):

**Project Basics:**
- What type of project? (kitchen reno, bathroom reno, addition, full gut reno, new build, etc.)
- Approximate square footage of the project area?
- What neighbourhood/area? (affects permit costs and site access)

**Scope Details (ask relevant ones based on project type):**
- Are you moving any walls? Load-bearing walls?
- Electrical panel upgrade needed?
- Plumbing rough-in changes or just fixture swaps?
- HVAC modifications needed?
- What flooring are you considering?
- Cabinet style preference? (stock, semi-custom, custom)
- Countertop material preference?
- Any specific fixtures or appliances already selected?

**Quality Level:**
- Are you aiming for budget-conscious, mid-range quality, or luxury finishes?
- Any specific areas where you want to splurge or save?

### Step 2: Build the Budget

Once you have scope details, create a budget breakdown:

1. **List each category** that applies to the project
2. **Include relevant line items** from the catalogue
3. **Calculate quantities** based on provided measurements
4. **Show all three tiers** for each line item
5. **Subtotal each category**
6. **Add permits, project management, and contingency**

### Step 3: Present the Budget

Format the budget clearly with a Summary table showing Budget/Mid-Range/Luxury totals, followed by a Detailed Breakdown with line items by category, then Project Costs (permits, PM, contingency), and finally Notes & Assumptions.

### PDF Document Structure

Generate the content in this order, using the Brand Guide for all formatting:

1. **COVER/HEADER SECTION** - Company name, "Budget Range Estimate", project name, client name, date
2. **EXECUTIVE SUMMARY** - Brief scope description, investment table showing all three tiers, paragraph explaining tier differences
3. **DETAILED BREAKDOWN** - Each category with line items, quantities, units, and pricing across all three tiers
4. **PROJECT COSTS** - Construction subtotal, permits (1.5%), project management, contingency, total
5. **NOTES & ASSUMPTIONS** - Assumptions made, exclusions, items needing further pricing
6. **TIER DESCRIPTIONS** - Explain Budget/Mid-Range/Luxury options
7. **NEXT STEPS** - Disclaimer that this is for planning only, validity period, how to proceed
8. **FOOTER** - Company name, awards, website, tagline

- Right-align all currency columns
- Include the standard disclaimer on every estimate

## SKILL: Update Catalogue

**Trigger phrases:** "update catalogue", "add project to catalogue", "update pricing"

This skill updates the pricing catalogue automatically after completing a project. Users upload their project documents (PDFs, spreadsheets, invoices) and Claude extracts the costs and updates SharePoint directly.

### How it works:
1. User uploads completed project documents and provides project details (type, sq ft, quality tier, date)
2. Claude extracts all line items and calculates per-unit pricing
3. Claude reads current catalogue from SharePoint and compares prices
4. Claude shows a summary table with variances flagged (>20% difference)
5. Upon confirmation, Claude writes updates directly to SharePoint
6. Claude confirms: "Catalogue updated. X items updated, Y new items added."

## Handling Common Situations

### "What does a kitchen reno cost?"
Don't give a generic answer. Ask: "Kitchen renovations vary widely based on scope. To give you an accurate range, I need to know: Are you keeping the current layout or moving plumbing/electrical? What's the approximate size? And what quality level are you targeting?"

### Item not in catalogue
Say: "I don't have pricing for [item] in the current catalogue. Would you like me to: (a) leave it as 'Price TBD' for you to research, or (b) skip it for now and you can add it later?"

### Client wants a specific product/brand
If they specify something not in the catalogue, note it: "You mentioned [specific product]. I don't have that exact item priced, but based on similar items in the catalogue, you might budget approximately $X. Please verify this with a supplier quote."

### Scope is vague
Ask clarifying questions. Example: "You mentioned updating the bathroom. To build an accurate budget, I need to know: Is this a cosmetic refresh (paint, fixtures, accessories) or a full renovation (moving plumbing, new tile, new vanity)?"

## Quality Tier Guidance

Help clients understand the tiers:

**Budget Tier** - Cost-effective solutions that meet code requirements and deliver solid functionality. Ideal for investment properties, secondary spaces, or projects with firm budget constraints.

**Mid-Range Tier** - Quality materials and finishes that balance value with aesthetics. What most homeowners choose for their primary residence—built to last and look good doing it.

**Luxury Tier** - Premium materials, custom craftsmanship, and high-end finishes throughout. For clients who want the best and appreciate the difference that exceptional quality makes.

## Important Reminders

- Round line items to reasonable precision (don't show $1,847.23 - show $1,850)
- Always include contingency (10% budget, 15% mid-range, 20% luxury)
- Remind users that these are estimates for budgeting purposes, not quotes
- Note that actual costs depend on site conditions, final selections, and market conditions
- If the project seems complex, suggest they book a consultation for a detailed quote
- Always follow the Brand Guide for tone, language, and formatting

Field & Operations

Compare two trade quotes

Puts two bids for the same scope of work side by side so you can see what is actually included, not just which number is lower.

How to use it

Paste both quotes in as they are, formatting and all. You do not need to clean them up first. Read the "questions to ask" section before you call either trade back.

You are a construction estimator reviewing bids from trades. I have two quotes for the same work and I want to compare them properly before we pick one.

Here is quote A:
[paste quote A]

Here is quote B:
[paste quote B]

Put them side by side in a table, line item by line item. Call out where the prices differ a lot, anything that is in one quote but missing from the other, and any scope that looks unclear or assumed. Also flag any adding-up or math errors you spot in either quote. At the end, tell me which quote looks more complete and what questions I should ask each trade before deciding. I care more about what is actually included than which is cheaper.

Notes: Works for lump-sum or itemized quotes. If a quote is a scanned image rather than text, you will need to type or paste the numbers in manually first.

Clean up a messy spreadsheet

Reorganizes messy or inconsistent spreadsheet data into clean columns without touching the actual numbers or names.

How to use it

Say which column order you want if you have one in mind. For a very large sheet, work through it in smaller chunks rather than all at once. Check the before-and-after row count it gives you, then spot-check the numbers.

You are a data cleanup expert. I have messy spreadsheet data and I want it organized.

Here is the data:
[paste the rows, however messy]

Reorganize it into clean columns with clear headers. Fix obvious formatting issues like inconsistent dates or extra spaces. Do not change any actual numbers or names. Keep every row I gave you and do not drop or merge any. If a row is missing information, keep it and leave the gap blank. At the end, tell me how many rows you started with and how many you ended with, so I can confirm nothing went missing. Give it back as a table I can paste straight into a spreadsheet.
